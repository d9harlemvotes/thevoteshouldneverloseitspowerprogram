Opening Remarks

Jannine Rowser, Esq.

President, Sterling Johnson, Jr. New York Chapter, National Black Prosecutors Association

Our chapter’s name carries a history worth sharing. The Honorable Sterling Johnson, Jr. served as New York City’s Special Narcotics Prosecutor and later as a federal judge. But he also helped found the National Black Prosecutors Association, bringing Black prosecutors together to build professional connections, mentorship, and opportunity.

Our chapter bears his name in recognition of that legacy. Judge Johnson understood that public service involves more than a title. It means opening doors for others and remembering the communities our institutions are meant to serve.

That legacy speaks to our presence here tonight. Laws and public decisions affect everyday lives, and people deserve the information they need to understand them and ask informed questions.

On behalf of our chapter, I thank D9 Harlem Votes® and Alpha Kappa Alpha Sorority, Incorporated®, Pi Kappa Omega Chapter, for this partnership, and the Schomburg Center for welcoming us.

We are pleased to be part of this conversation. Thank you.

Introducing R.E.A.C.T.S.

Erika L. Ewing

Co-Chair and Founding Member, D9 Harlem Votes® Steering Committee
Lead Curriculum Developer

R.E.A.C.T.S.

  • RRemoving a requirement
  • EExpanding authority
  • AAdding a requirement
  • CCentralizing a process
  • TTransferring authority
  • SSimplifying or shortening a process

From discussion to framework. R.E.A.C.T.S. began inside our coalition. When this year’s ballot proposals were released, D9 Harlem Votes® members met to study them, reading the official language line by line and asking what each proposal would change, what would happen if it passed, and what would stay the same. From those discussions, we developed R.E.A.C.T.S., a structured framework and an interactive, nonpartisan civic engagement experience for our community.

What it is. Ballot proposals appear on the back of the ballot, written in the formal language of the City Charter. R.E.A.C.T.S. is a reading framework that names six kinds of change a proposal can make.

Why we use it. A proposal’s title tells you its subject, but the change it would make is often harder to see. Naming that change gives every reader the same starting point, whatever their background, and lets each of us judge a proposal by what it would do. The framework is nonpartisan. It does not point anyone toward a “yes” or a “no.” Your Choice. Your Voice. Your Vote.

How it works. For each proposal, read the official summary, then the explanations of a “yes” vote and a “no” vote. Ask what would be different if the proposal passed, then choose the letter, or letters, that describe that change.

What you will do tonight. Our Civic Engagement Hosts will lead a practice round, then walk through all five New York City ballot proposals. Read along on the screen or in this program, and under each “Identify the Change” slide, tap the letters you choose. Your answers stay on your phone. Talk with the people sitting near you and compare what you found. Our hosts will also come to your seats to hear how Harlem R.E.A.C.T.S. The aim is shared understanding of the language, of the change, and of what each proposal could mean for you, for your neighbors, and for our community. No one has to agree.

R.E.A.C.T.S. developed by Erika L. Ewing, M.F.A., and the D9 Harlem Votes® Interactive Engagement Team

The Vote Should Never Lose Its Power

Roundtable Overview

Jannine Rowser, Esq.

President, Sterling Johnson, Jr. New York Chapter, National Black Prosecutors Association

When our chapter debuted its voting-rights panel discussion on Juneteenth, one thing became clear: this conversation needed to continue, reaching more people and creating greater opportunities for community engagement.

Through our partnership with Alpha Kappa Alpha Sorority, Incorporated®, Pi Kappa Omega Chapter, and D9 Harlem Votes®, the next conversation began to take shape, incorporating New York City’s ballot proposals.

When plans for tonight’s program changed, we forged ahead and brought the conversation right here to Harlem, the heartbeat of Black American history and culture.

In 1965, the Voting Rights Act confronted discriminatory barriers to voting. Sixty-one years later, questions of voting rights, representation, and access remain critically important.

Tonight, we’re bringing different generations and perspectives together, not for a series of speeches, but for a meaningful exchange about our shared responsibility to protect the power of the vote.

Our discussion tonight comes back to one principle: The Vote Should Never Lose Its Power.

To begin our panelist and moderator introductions, please welcome my colleague, Joel Wright, Esq., Vice President of Membership of the Sterling Johnson, Jr. New York Chapter of the National Black Prosecutors Association.

Panelist Biography

Zsa’Queria B. Martin, Esq.

Zsa’Queria Martin is an attorney, political strategist, and democracy advocate originally from Greenville, South Carolina, and raised in Georgia. She earned her undergraduate degree from Clark Atlanta University and her law degree from Florida A&M University College of Law. After moving to New York City, she practiced criminal defense at The Bronx Defenders. She now does impact litigation with Democracy Defenders Fund and leads Lilly J. Strategies, while also serving as Civic Engagement Co-Chair for the NAACP Mid-Manhattan Branch. Her work spans legal advocacy, political strategy, and civic engagement, with a focus on protecting rights and strengthening democratic participation.

Panelist Biography

Charles Johnson, Esq.

Charles Johnson, Esq., is an attorney with more than a decade of experience in employment and statutory law, mainly involving collective bargaining negotiations, administrative trials, arbitrations, and labor relations. Charles was also a law clerk at the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission headquarters in Washington, D.C., under the Obama administration. Currently, Charles works for Cornell University’s ILR School in its Labor and Employment Law Program as an educator, building curriculum and teaching courses on New York State labor and employment laws. Charles served as First Vice Chair of Manhattan’s Community Board 10 (Central Harlem) and currently chairs the Civic Engagement Committee for the NAACP Mid-Manhattan Branch. Charles also serves on the Resolution Committee and as an Executive Committee member for the NAACP New York State Conference. Lastly, Charles is the Political Action Chair for the NAACP New York State Conference.

Panelist Biography

Naila Keys

Naila Keys is a student at Pace University, majoring in Writing for Equity and Diversity in Theater and Media, with a minor in Peace and Justice Studies.

She is a member of Alpha Kappa Alpha Sorority, Incorporated® (Rho Theta Chapter), where she serves as Parliamentarian and Sisterly Relations Head. She also serves as Assistant Copy Editor and Secretary of Fusion Magazine, contributing her writing, editorial, and organizational skills to collegiate creative expression.

Naila’s academic and creative work explores social justice, representation, and the power of storytelling. She has directed projects addressing issues affecting Black women, including fatphobia and misogynoir, and has facilitated educational discussions on diversity, equity, and inclusion in American entertainment.

Panelist Biography

Honorable Milton A. Tingling

The Honorable Milton A. Tingling is a trailblazing jurist, former New York State Supreme Court Justice, and longtime civic leader whose career is deeply rooted in Harlem.

In 1996, he made history with his election to the New York City Civil Court in a judicial district encompassing Harlem and Washington Heights. He later served on the New York State Supreme Court and became the first African American County Clerk in New York State, serving as New York County Clerk, Commissioner of Jurors, and Clerk of the Supreme Court.

Judge Tingling’s commitment to civic participation extends beyond the courtroom. Through his work with The Initiative, he has helped formerly incarcerated New Yorkers understand their voting eligibility and the restoration of other civil rights. His judicial career has also included significant decisions involving government authority and accountability under the New York City Charter.

He is part of a family spanning three generations of Black judges and has spoken about his firsthand experiences of segregation while growing up in South Carolina.

Moderator Biography

Nigel L. Farinha, Esq.

Nigel L. Farinha, Esq., is a veteran New York City prosecutor and legal leader who has served in the Office of the Special Narcotics Prosecutor since 1996. His leadership roles include Chief Diversity, Equity & Inclusion Officer, Chairman of the Conviction Review Committee, and Chief of Gang Prosecutions.

A former president of the local chapter of the National Black Prosecutors Association, Farinha has served on the New York State Bar Association’s Task Force on Racial Justice and Equity and has contributed to legal education, professional mentoring, and youth leadership initiatives.

He received the New York City Bar Association’s 2017 Thomas E. Dewey Medal for outstanding contributions to the Office of the District Attorney. He earned his undergraduate degree from Brown University and his Juris Doctor from New York University School of Law.
